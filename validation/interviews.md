# User Interviews

To validate the idea behind the **Injury-Aware Workout Planner**, we conducted interviews with individuals representing both **medical expertise** and **real user experience**. The goal was to understand whether people would trust AI-assisted workout adaptation and what concerns or expectations might arise.


## Interview 1: Neha Agarwal  
**Role:** Medical Professional – Senior Resident

I discussed whether medical professionals would be comfortable with people using AI tools to generate exercise recommendations.

### Key Insights

- Doctors generally recommend **physiotherapy-based movements rather than heavy workouts** when a person is recovering from an injury.
- She emphasized the importance of **including medical disclaimers** before suggesting any exercise recommendations.
- From a medical perspective, AI-assisted guidance can be acceptable **if proper safety precautions and disclaimers are included**.
- She noted that many people will likely **rely on AI tools for guidance**, which makes it important for such systems to be designed responsibly.
- After hearing the concept and workflow of the application, she mentioned that the idea is **novel and interesting**, and she would be curious to see how the final product develops.

### Takeaway

Medical professionals may be open to AI-assisted tools **as long as they emphasize safety, include disclaimers, and prioritize physiotherapy-style exercises over intense workouts during recovery**.


## Interview 2: Aman Sharma  
**Role:** Gym Enthusiast with Shoulder Injury Experience

To understand the user perspective, we interviewed a regular gym-goer who had previously experienced shoulder injuries.

### Key Insights

- During his injury recovery, he mainly relied on **light exercises and physiotherapy movements rather than intense workouts**.
- This suggested that future versions of the application should **include physiotherapy-oriented alternatives alongside workout substitutions**.
- One of the biggest challenges during injury recovery is **figuring out which exercises to skip or modify in workout videos**.
- Currently, users often need to **manually search for safer alternatives and adjust workouts themselves**.
- He mentioned that a tool that automatically **flags risky exercises and suggests safer options** would be very helpful.

### Takeaway

Users recovering from injuries would benefit from a system that **automatically identifies unsafe exercises and suggests safer alternatives**, removing the need to manually modify workouts.


## Interview 3: Raghav Narula
**Role:** Musculoskeletal Physiotherapist at Vita Health Group UK

We spoke with a practising physiotherapist to understand how rehab and exercise prescription work in practice, where current tools fall short, and what would make AI-based rehab tools clinically acceptable and useful.

### Key Insights

**Clinical accuracy & reliability**

- There are no widely used “AI” rehab tools in practice; clinicians rely on **exercise libraries** (e.g. Physi-track) to send programs; generic running apps (e.g. Runna) and YouTube/Google are still common, and many patients present only after injury.
- Digital tools often **do not track range of motion** well without wearables; phone cameras can help. **Exercise adherence** is a major gap and a key measure that needs to be strengthened (e.g. literature on PubMed around “exercise adherence” and “exercise apps”).
- Before prescribing strengthening, **functional screening** matters: sit-to-stand, single-leg comfort, double-leg hopping, stair climbing. **Outcome measures** are injury-specific (e.g. KOOS for joint arthritis, Melbourne ACL for ACL).

**Workflow & time**

- The **subjective history** takes the most time; tools like Heidi (AI) can record conversations and generate notes. Phio is used for triaging and red flags. A common issue is patients trying to **skip surveys** to get to a professional faster.
- **Physi-track** is used for exercise prescription. Programs are **manually adjusted** only about 10% of the time. Face-to-face goal setting (e.g. PSFS) and **per-session scoring** are important for tracking progress.
- Ideal integration would combine **evidence-based content** (e.g. E3 Rehab–style), **AI workout suggestions**, **progress tracking**, **goal setting**, and **user availability** (how much time they can give).

**Patient experience & adherence**

- Patients often fail to follow rehab because **expectations are not met**. Understanding of **load management and progression** is rare. **Pain scales** (VAS, NPRS) are widely used but patients struggle to report feedback accurately; only about **10%** would realistically log daily data.
- Trust in digital rehab is still emerging; factors include **accessibility** and **reduced waiting lists**.

**Safety & risk**

- **Back-pain-related** AI-generated plans were cited as a concern—they can be dangerous if not carefully designed. **Early-stage rehab** is when AI is least trusted; **injury-specific red-flag questions** should be able to pause or stop a program.
- **Human oversight** is especially important when progress is **not normal**. **Progress tracking** is critical from both clinical and liability perspectives.

**Mid-stage rehab**

- The **longest phase** of rehab is mid-stage; common mistakes are **returning to normal too soon** and expecting quick results. The same applies when transitioning from physio to gym—**returning too soon** is the main problem.
- Safe progression is **stage-wise**; risky progression is **returning to normal too soon**, including psycho-social factors. AI could potentially adjust **weekly load progression** under **controlled and chaos tests** for return to sport/activity.

**Cost & adoption**

- **Cost and access** are real barriers (e.g. ~£90 for 30 minutes). Clinics would adopt AI tools **if they save time and integrate with existing systems**—e.g. after early rehab, switching to an app for **mid-stage rehab**.
- A **subscription** model (e.g. weekly or 3-month) was suggested as sensible.

**Strategic & product**

- If one part could be safely automated, **mid-stage rehab** and **progress tracking** were highlighted, with data fed back to professionals to reduce their time. **Research-backed** content (not solely LLM knowledge) would increase comfort in recommending an AI rehab system. **Feedback from physicians** was noted as a very important feature to include.

### Takeaway

Physiotherapists would consider AI-based rehab tools **if they are research- and evidence-backed, integrate with existing workflows (e.g. EMR or clinic systems), and focus on mid-stage rehab with strong progress tracking and goal setting**. Safety must be prioritised: injury-specific red flags, avoidance of high-risk areas (e.g. generic back-pain plans), and human oversight when progress is not normal. **Physician feedback loops** and **exercise adherence** are key areas to strengthen for clinical acceptance and real-world impact.


## Interview 4: Rachit Singh
**Role:** Football Enthusiast with Knee Injury Experience

To understand the needs of athletes returning to activity after injury, we interviewed a football enthusiast who had previously experienced a knee injury.

### Key Insights

- During recovery, he focused primarily on **strengthening and stability exercises** recommended by his physiotherapist, especially for the quads, hamstrings, and knee stabilizers.
- He mentioned that **returning to sports too quickly** is a common mistake, as many athletes struggle to judge when their knee is ready for higher-impact activities like running, cutting, or jumping.
- One of the biggest challenges was **modifying training routines while still staying active**, since many football drills and lower-body workouts can put excessive stress on the knee joint.
- He often had to **search online** to determine whether certain exercises (like lunges, squats, or plyometrics) were safe during recovery, which was time-consuming and sometimes confusing.
- He said a tool that could **assess injury stage and recommend appropriate exercises with controlled progression** would be very useful.

### Takeaway

Athletes recovering from knee injuries would benefit from a system that **evaluates recovery stage and recommends safe lower-body exercises with gradual progression**, helping them stay active while reducing the risk of reinjury.
